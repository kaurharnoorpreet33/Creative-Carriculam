Curriculum Area 9: Humanities and Social Sciences (HASS)

Introduction · Theories · Resources · Learning experiences · Reflection

Introduction and Importance

Humanities and Social Sciences (HASS) members develop creativity by promoting cultural, identity, community, historical, citizenship, and environmental exploration in children. Children learn to be empathetic, problem-solving, and imaginative through storytelling, role-playing, mapping, and inquiry-based investigations. The second version of EYLF focuses on a sense of belonging, cultural identity, and cognition of the surrounding world (AGDE, 2022). Creative HASS learning enables children to perceive the community in various ways, express their ideas in a symbolic way and also act upon the social concepts through a game. Children develop skills of critical and creative thinking when they envision the roles in a community or they can compare the events in the past and in the present, and it makes their knowledge about the real-world context stronger.

Creativity Theories and Perspectives

According to the sociocultural theory as developed by Vygotsky, the innovative comprehension of children develops as a result of communication with others and cultural artefacts. The Playworlds approach by Fleer (2019) demonstrates that imaginative HASS spaces contribute to a better inquiry environment through fictional and real worlds mixing. Constructivist theories believe in children being active in creating an understanding of culture, identity and society. Sawyer has the ability to work collaboratively in terms of creativity, as we see when children bargain in social roles within a dramatic play in the community. Interpersonal and intrapersonal intelligences make Gardner empathetic and socially creative. These views combine to reveal that HASS fosters creativity through the integration of the power to visualise, enquire, and engage emotionally and critically.

Resources, Materials & Digital Technologies

Maps, globes, photographs, cultural artefacts, puppets, community role-play props, dress-ups, story baskets, and timelines can be given by teachers. Field observation and outdoor walks enrich real-world knowledge. There are inquiry opportunities like digital timelines, child-friendly mapping applications, virtual museum tours, and Google Earth, to name a few, all digital tools that enhance inquiry. Cameras and tablets can be used to record information about the community and make digital stories. Having culturally inclusive material will assist the children in expressing identity creativity, relating to the worldviews and learning about diversity in the surrounding world.

Learning Experiences

0–2 years
  • Community Faces Basket – Infants explore images of well-known people, creating a sense of initial identity awareness and understanding of valuable social interaction (Fleer, 2019).
  • Family Story Songs – The teacher sings family songs and name songs, which will assist the infants in building belonging, emotional safety and cultural belonging (AGDE, 2022).
2–3 years
  • Mini Community Play – Children interact with the toy houses, people, and cars and explore the new social roles and relations in the community creatively (Promoting Creativity, 2023).
  • My Place Photo Walk – Simple photographs of places known to the children will enhance early mapping and help them understand the surrounding world (Zhao, 2020).
3–5 years
  • Community Helpers Dramatic Play – Children act as doctors, builders, firefighters, etc., and learn more about the roles and duties of the community members (Fleer, 2019).
  • Culture Story Circle – Children tell or see cultural artefacts, and in the process, they acquire empathy, storytelling, and creative appreciation of a variety of traditions (Turda and Iuga, 2022).
6–8 years
  • Create-a-Map Challenge – Students create neighbourhood maps with symbols and legends, building spatial arguments and imaginative cartographic knowledge (AGDE, 2022).
  • Digital Museum Tour & Reflection – Children visit and learn about virtual museum exhibits and make creative digital posters about their learning experiences (Quigley et al., 2020).

Reflection and Evaluation – Humanities and Social Sciences

The HASS enactments succeeded in enhancing the social imagination of children, their cultural understanding and their expression. Embracing dramatic community play was one of the positive sides as covered by children. They were sure to lead in various roles, negotiate duties, and form plots – evidence of joint creativity termed by Vygotsky and Fleer (2019). The other area of strength was the involvement of children in the mapping task; they were creative with symbols and made combinations of real-world experiences in their drawings which reflected flexible thinking.

Nevertheless, it could have improved in some areas. Certain children struggled with abstract aspects of HASS, like roles in the community, without any tangible examples, which is why scaffolding should be stronger. Moreover, online museum tasks needed to be more directed so as not to get distracted.

In case of repeating the experiences, I would start with more vivid visual stimulation, e.g., photos, brief videos, and artefacts to create the background knowledge. I would also implement step-by-step modelling of digital exploration and templates of map symbols. Metacognition would be enhanced by the inclusion of circles of reflection where the children discuss their reasoning. Such changes would enhance knowledge, advance the ability to enquire, and build creative incorporation of concepts of HASS.
